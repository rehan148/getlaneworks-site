What a complete import quote to New Zealand includes

3 November 2026 · All articles

In short: Sea freight to Auckland or Tauranga, the customs entry, the Customs and MPI goods levy, biosecurity checks, 15% GST and door delivery, with a worked example.

Quoting an import into New Zealand looks a lot like quoting one into Australia, and that's where mistakes creep in. The freight legs are similar, but the border charges, the biosecurity process and the GST rate are all different. A complete NZ import quote separates the freight, the destination port charges, the customs and MPI side, the duty and GST the importer pays to Customs, and door delivery, and says what could be added later.

1. Sea freight to Auckland or Tauranga

Start with the port. Auckland and Tauranga are the usual discharge ports for the upper North Island, and the choice affects the quote more than the ocean rate does. If the consignee is in Auckland and the carrier discharges in Tauranga, you need a rail or road leg to get the box north, and that has to be in the price or clearly excluded. State the routing, transit time, and whether the rate is all-in.

As with any import, the Incoterm decides where your quote starts. On FOB you quote from the ship's rail at origin. On EXW you also quote pickup, export clearance and origin terminal charges. Our guide to Incoterms 2020 for forwarders covers which charges belong to which side.

2. Destination port and terminal charges

List the destination charges line by line, not as one lump: terminal handling, delivery order or documentation fee, and for LCL the CFS unpack and handling charges. Say which currency each line is in and what exchange rate you used for any total.

3. The customs import entry and Customs/MPI levies

Commercial consignments valued over NZ$1,000 are treated as high-value imports, and Customs collects duty and GST on them. Below that threshold, Customs generally won't collect duty or GST unless the goods are alcohol or tobacco (NZ Customs, duty and GST FAQs). The same page notes that the importer needs a Customs client number for shipments valued at NZ$1,000 or more, so ask for it early or allow time to register.

The import entry is usually lodged by a customs broker on the importer's behalf. Your quote should show the brokerage fee and what it covers, typically a set number of tariff lines with a per-line charge above that.

Then the government levies. From 1 April 2026 Customs and MPI collect a combined goods levy on each high-value import. For sea cargo it's NZ$73.87 (Customs) plus NZ$44.57 (MPI), a total of NZ$118.44 excluding GST. For air cargo it's NZ$51.81 excluding GST (NZ Customs, goods fees and hourly rate). Customs says the broker or forwarder usually pays the levy and may pass it on (NZ Customs), so show it as a pass-through at cost.

4. MPI biosecurity: clearance, checks and possible inspection

Every import has to meet MPI's import health standards for the type of container, the cargo and the packaging (MPI, steps to importing containers and cargo). In practice the quote needs to cover three things.

  • Biosecurity clearance on the entry. Your broker answers the biosecurity questions with the import entry. Ask about the goods, and also about timber packaging, pallets and dunnage, because packaging alone can trigger requirements.
  • Unpacking at a transitional facility. Containers that are unpacked at an MPI-approved transitional facility are checked by an accredited person, who must complete basic biosecurity training, and the facility is audited by MPI (MPI, sea container checks at transitional facilities). If the consignee's site isn't a transitional facility, that changes where the box can go and may add a devanning charge.
  • Seasonal measures. MPI applies brown marmorated stink bug measures to certain goods from risk countries during the risk season, and says the highest risk is generally from October to December (MPI, BMSB requirements). For vehicles, machinery and parts in particular, check whether offshore treatment is needed before you quote.

Inspections, treatments and any extra handling can't be predicted at quote time. Don't put them in the total. List them as possible extras and say they're charged at cost if they arise.

5. Duty and GST

Duty depends on the tariff classification and origin. If the goods qualify under a free trade agreement and the importer holds valid origin documentation, the preferential rate may apply. Give an estimate, state the rate you assumed, and say it's confirmed on entry.

GST on imports in New Zealand is 15%, calculated on the value of the goods plus any duty, plus freight and insurance charges. Values in foreign currency are converted at the Customs exchange rate for the day the goods arrive, not the purchase date, and Customs won't release the goods until duty and GST are paid (NZ Customs). That date rule is worth a line in your quote, because the importer's final GST figure can move with the exchange rate.

For a GST-registered importer, the GST paid to Customs isn't a final cost. Inland Revenue says it can be claimed back if the business is GST registered and uses the goods solely to make taxable supplies (IRD, claiming GST). It's still cash out before release, so if you pay it on the importer's behalf, say so and show any disbursement fee.

6. Door delivery

Quote cartage from the port or transitional facility to the consignee's address, with the suburb and postcode you priced. State what's included: side-loader or tipper, free time for unloading, and whether the empty return is in the price.

Worked example

1 x 20GP, FOB Ningbo to Auckland, door delivery to East Tāmaki 2013, three tariff lines, goods valued over NZ$1,000. Freight, local and cartage figures are example rates. The levy is the published amount above.
