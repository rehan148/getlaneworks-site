How to structure a freight rate sheet your team (and software) can use

2 November 2026 · By the Laneworks team · All articles

In short: Seven tabs (FCL, LCL, air, local charges, door zones, customs, rules) and the columns that let anyone quote from it.

In most small forwarders the rates exist, but not in one place. Ocean rates are in a carrier's email from last Tuesday, local charges in a spreadsheet someone built in 2022, cartage zones in a transport company's PDF, and the clearance fee in the ops manager's head. That works while one person quotes. It breaks when they're on leave, and it makes any kind of automation impossible.

A good rate sheet is one workbook that a new staff member could quote from on their first day, and that software can read without guessing. Here's a structure that does both.

Rules that apply to every tab

  • One price per row. No "A$1,200 / A$1,350" in a single cell.
  • Units in their own column. Per container, per W/M, per kg, per bill, per pallet, per line. Never only in a heading.
  • Currency in its own column. USD ocean rates and AUD local charges can sit in the same sheet if each row says which.
  • Valid from and valid to dates on every row that expires, as real dates, not "Nov".
  • No merged cells, no colour coding as data, no prices in comments. If a colour means something, make it a column.
  • Codes, not nicknames. UN/LOCODEs or IATA airport codes for places (CNNGB, AUMEL, PVG), standard container codes (20GP, 40GP, 40HC).

Tab 1: FCL ocean

Columns: origin port, destination port, container type, rate, currency, carrier, transit time, valid from, valid to, surcharges included (yes/no), notes. If surcharges are listed separately, put each one on its own row with the same port pair and a type column, rather than in extra columns that half the rows leave blank.

Tab 2: LCL ocean

Columns: origin, destination, rate per W/M, minimum W/M, currency, consolidator, valid from, valid to. Put origin and destination CFS charges on the local charges tab, not here, so they can differ by port without copying rows. The calculation these rates feed is in how to calculate an LCL quote.

Tab 3: Air

One row per origin, destination and weight break: minimum, under 45 kg, +45, +100, +300, +500, +1000. Columns: origin airport, destination airport, break, rate per kg, minimum charge, currency, airline, valid from, valid to. Add a column for the volumetric divisor if you buy from carriers that use different ones. Fuel and security surcharges go on their own rows, per chargeable kilo.

Tab 4: Local charges

This is the tab most sheets get wrong. Every charge at origin or destination, one row each. Columns: port or airport, direction (import or export), mode (FCL, LCL, air), charge name, basis (per container, per W/M, per bill, per kg), container type if it applies, amount, minimum, currency, GST applies (yes/no), and a condition column: always, if clearance, if EXW, if DG, if inspected. That condition column is what turns a list of charges into something you can quote from without remembering which ones apply.

Tab 5: Door delivery zones

Two parts. First, a postcode table: postcode from, postcode to, zone. Second, a price table: port or airport, zone, equipment (20, 40, side-loader, pallet, tailgate), basis, amount, waiting time allowance, waiting time rate. Pricing by postcode rather than suburb avoids the ambiguity of suburb names and matches how most transport companies already work.

If your carrier passes on terminal access charges, add them here or on the local charges tab, and decide once which line they belong on.

Tab 6: Customs and compliance

Your brokerage fee, any fee for extra tariff lines over a set number, the fee for lodging a biosecurity import declaration, permit handling, and government charges marked "at cost" with a link to the current schedule, because they change each financial year. Duty and import GST aren't rates; they're calculated per shipment and passed through.

Tab 7: Rules

The things you know but haven't written down.

  • The exchange rate you use for quotes and when you update it.
  • Your margin on each mode, or whether rates in the sheet already include it.
  • Default validity for each mode.
  • Your standard exclusions paragraph, word for word.
  • Defaults when the request is incomplete: "If no Incoterm, quote FOB. If no postcode, quote to port."
  • Your floor: below which a person must approve the price.

Keep it current

A rate sheet is only useful if it's trusted. Give one person the job of updating it, and do it on a fixed day. When a rate expires, don't delete it: move it to an archive tab so you can see what you quoted last month. Highlight rows that expire in the next seven days with a formula, not by hand.

Why this helps software too

Everything above is what software needs to quote from your rates instead of guessing. Clear units, explicit conditions and real dates are the difference between a tool that drafts a correct quote and one that drafts a plausible one. It's also what makes freight quote automation worth starting: if the rates are clean, reading the email is the only hard part left.
