AI for freight forwarders in 2026: what actually works today

2 November 2026 · All articles

In short: Reading unstructured email, extracting packing lists and invoices, drafting quotes from your own rates, and what still needs a human.

Most writing about AI in freight is either a vendor's promise or a consultant's forecast. Neither helps the owner of a ten-person forwarder decide what to try on Monday. This article sticks to work that current tools do reliably, work they do with supervision, and work they shouldn't be trusted with yet. No market sizes, no predictions.

What it does well: reading unstructured email

The biggest change in the last few years is that software can now read a badly written email the way an experienced operator would. "Hi, need price for 2 x 40 from Ningbo, FOB, to our warehouse in Dandenong, cleared please" contains a container type, a quantity, a port, an Incoterm, a delivery suburb and a clearance instruction. Older rule-based tools needed a form to get that. Current language models get it from the email, including the forwarded thread, the signature block and the reply that says "sorry, make that 3".

That matters because reading is where much of the quoting time goes. It's also where the errors start: a missed "HC", a delivery address in the second email of a thread, an Incoterm that changed halfway through.

What it does well: pulling data from documents

Packing lists, commercial invoices and supplier spreadsheets arrive as PDFs and Excel files in a dozen layouts. Extracting carton counts, dimensions, gross weights and invoice values from them used to mean retyping. Current tools handle mixed layouts well, including scanned PDFs, and can total cartons and cubic metres across a packing list.

Two cautions. First, a document can be wrong at the source: a supplier's packing list that says 0.5 cbm for a pallet that's clearly 1.2 is still wrong after extraction. Second, totals should be shown next to the line items they came from, so a person can spot a carton row that was read twice.

What it does with supervision: drafting quotes

Given a structured request and your rate sheet, drafting a quote is lookup and arithmetic. The model reads the request; ordinary code finds the rows and does the sums. That split is important. A language model should not be making up a freight rate or doing currency conversion in its head. It should be choosing which row of your sheet applies, and the numbers should come from the sheet.

Done that way, a draft for a standard request is usually right, and when it's wrong it's wrong in a way you can see: a different port pair, a missed zone, a container size misread. A person still checks every draft before it goes out. The point is that checking a draft is much faster than writing one. Our article on freight quote automation covers which requests to start with.

What it also does with supervision: the follow-up questions

When a request is missing the delivery postcode or the dimensions, a model can list exactly what's missing and write the question email. This is low-risk and high-value: it gets something back to the customer the same hour, which matters more than people think (see why quote response time decides who wins the freight).

What still needs a person

  • Commercial decisions. Discounting to win a lane, holding price for a good customer, walking away from a bad one.
  • Tariff classification and customs declarations. In Australia, only the owner of the goods or a licensed customs broker can enter goods for home consumption (ABF, about customs brokers). A model can suggest a classification; the broker is accountable for it.
  • Dangerous goods and special cargo. Lithium batteries, oversize pieces and temperature control carry consequences that make a fast guess the wrong trade.
  • Exceptions on live shipments. Rolled bookings, short shipments and inspections need someone who knows the carrier and the customer.
  • Anything sent to a customer. A draft should never send itself.

Where it goes wrong

The failure modes are predictable, which makes them manageable.

  • Confident gaps. A model asked to quote will quote. If the request doesn't say whether clearance is needed, it should say so, not quietly assume. Look for tools that list their assumptions.
  • Out-of-date rates. The model is only as current as your rate sheet. Expired rows should be flagged, not used.
  • Hidden working. If you can't see which rate row produced a number, you'll end up checking everything by hand and saving nothing.
  • Data handling. Customer emails contain commercial information. Ask any vendor where your data is processed, whether it's used to train models, and how long it's kept.

Where to start

Start where the work is most repetitive and the risk is lowest: incoming quote requests on lanes you already price. It needs no integration, because it works on email you already receive, and it doesn't touch your freight system's records. If it saves time on quoting, move on to document extraction for bookings. Leave customs and live exceptions until last.

Run it alongside your current process for two weeks. Quote the way you always do, compare the drafts, and count how many you'd have sent with no edits or one. That number, on your own requests and your own rates, is the only evaluation that matters.
