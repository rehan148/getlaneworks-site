Freight quote automation: what it is and what to automate first

2 November 2026 · All articles

In short: The five steps in every quote, why intake and assembly come before the rate lookup, how to measure it, and which decisions stay with a person.

"Quote automation" gets used for three different things: an online rate calculator on your website, a pricing engine inside your freight system, and software that reads a customer's email and drafts the reply. For a small forwarder these solve different problems, and only one of them deals with how most of your quote requests actually arrive, which is as an email from a customer who didn't fill in a form.

This article breaks a quote into its steps, says which step to automate first and why, and lists what shouldn't be automated at all.

The five steps in every quote

Whether it takes you five minutes or forty, every quote goes through the same five steps.

  • Intake. Read the request and work out what's being asked: mode, origin, destination, container or dimensions and weight, Incoterm, delivery address, whether clearance is needed.
  • Gap check. Notice what's missing and decide whether to quote with a stated assumption or ask first.
  • Rate lookup. Find the ocean or air rate, the origin or destination charges, the cartage zone and the clearance fee.
  • Assembly. Do the arithmetic, convert currency, add margin, and write it up with a total, validity, GST treatment and exclusions.
  • Review and send. Check the numbers against the request, then send it from your own mailbox.

The rate lookup is the step most people picture when they hear "automation". It's also the step that matters least on its own. A pricing engine can't price a request it hasn't been given in structured form, and someone still has to read the email, work out that "a container from Ningbo" means one 40HC FOB, and type that into the system.

What to automate first: intake and assembly together

Automate the steps either side of the lookup, not just the lookup. In practice that means starting with the first bucket of requests: a lane you already have a rate for, with enough detail to price it. These are the most common requests and the most mechanical.

For those requests, automation should do four things:

  • Read the email and any attached packing list or commercial invoice, and pull out the fields you'd write on a job sheet.
  • Look up each line in your own rate sheet, not a market average, so the price is the one you'd have quoted.
  • Write the reply in your format, with lines, a total, validity, GST treatment and exclusions.
  • Show its working: which rate-sheet row each line came from, and what it read from the email, so checking takes a couple of minutes.

The last point is what makes it safe. A quote with a wrong number in it costs more than a slow one. If the draft shows where each number came from, a person can check it against the request quickly and with confidence. If it doesn't, they'll recheck everything by hand and save nothing.

Automate the gap check second

The second bucket is requests on a standard lane with details missing: no delivery postcode, no Incoterm, "a few pallets" with no dimensions. Here the useful output isn't a quote, it's the question email, with every missing item asked at once and a default stated. "If I don't hear back I'll quote FOB, port to door." Software can spot the gaps reliably once it has read the request properly, and a numbered list of questions sent the same hour is worth more than a guessed quote sent tomorrow.

Leave these to a person

Some steps should stay manual, however good the software gets.

  • Pricing below your floor. Deciding to cut margin to win a lane or keep a customer is a commercial judgement.
  • Carrier and routing choice when it isn't already fixed in your rate sheet.
  • Dangerous goods, out-of-gauge and project cargo. The cost of getting these wrong is not a revised quote.
  • Credit terms and anything to do with a customer's account.
  • Tariff classification. A tool can suggest one, but the broker who lodges the declaration owns it.
  • Sending. Nothing should go to a customer unless a person has read it and pressed send.

Get your rates into one place before you start

Automation can only quote from rates it can read. If your ocean rates are in one spreadsheet, local charges in another, cartage zones in a transport company's PDF and the clearance fee in someone's head, the first job is putting them in one sheet with clear units and validity dates. That's worth doing even if you never automate anything, because it's also what lets a second person in your office quote. There's a guide to structuring a rate sheet that both your team and software can use.

How to tell if it's working

Pick three numbers to track before you start, and look at them after a month.

  • Time to first reply on each request, quote or question, from the email arriving to your reply leaving.
  • Edits per draft: how many lines a person changed before sending. If that number doesn't fall, the rate sheet is incomplete or the tool is misreading requests.
  • Requests quoted the same day as a share of all requests received.

Win rate matters too, but it moves slowly and depends on your prices. These three tell you within a few weeks whether the automation is taking the minutes out.

Email-first, not portal-first

A common mistake is to start with a customer quote portal. Portals are useful for large shippers with steady volumes, but most importers who buy from small forwarders will keep emailing, because that's how they've always done it and because their request often includes an attachment from their supplier. Automation that works on the email you already receive needs no change from your customers and no new screen for your staff. It also sits alongside whatever freight system you use.
