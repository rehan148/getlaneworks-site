Why quote response time decides who wins the freight (and how to get under an hour)

2 November 2026 · By the Laneworks team · All articles

In short: How importers actually buy freight, why the first complete reply sets the benchmark, and a practical process for replying within the hour.

There's no shortage of claims online about how much faster replies win more business, and most of them don't link to anything. This article doesn't need them. If you look at how an importer actually buys freight, the case for speed is plain, and so is the reason it matters more for small forwarders than large ones.

How importers buy freight

Think about the person sending you the request. They're usually a buyer, an ops coordinator or a business owner. Freight isn't their job; it's a step between paying a supplier and selling the goods. Their supplier has told them the cargo will be ready on a date. They email two or three forwarders, sometimes the same email to all of them, and wait.

From there, three things tend to happen.

  • The first complete reply becomes the benchmark. When the next quote arrives, it gets compared line by line with the one already open. If yours is first, the others are measured against your structure and your total.
  • A good-enough quote often gets booked before the rest arrive. The importer has a cargo ready date and a dozen other jobs. If the first quote is clear, sensible and in the right range, there's little reason to wait for a fourth.
  • Silence reads as a signal. A forwarder who takes a day to quote is assumed to take a day to answer when a container is held at the wharf. Importers choose on price, but they're also choosing who they'll have to chase.

None of this needs a statistic. It's how most people buy anything they don't enjoy buying.

What the research says, and doesn't

The most-cited study on response speed is a 2011 Harvard Business Review article on online sales leads, which found that most companies weren't responding nearly fast enough to enquiries (Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington, HBR, 2011). It's about inbound web leads across industries, not freight, and we haven't found published research on forwarder quote turnaround specifically. So treat it as consistent with the argument above, not as proof of a number.

Why it matters more for small forwarders

A large forwarder has a quoting desk and a brand. A small one has a director who quotes between other jobs and a reputation for being responsive. Speed is one of the few things a small forwarder can beat a large one on with no extra cost per quote, and it's also the first thing to slip on a busy day. The aim is a process that keeps the reply time short even when nobody has a spare half hour.

"Under an hour" means something goes back

The target isn't a full quote within an hour of every email. It's that every request gets a useful reply within an hour. That reply is one of three things.

  • A complete quote, for a standard lane where you have all the details.
  • A numbered list of questions, with defaults stated, when details are missing. "If I don't hear back I'll quote FOB, port to door."
  • A holding reply with a time, for anything non-standard: "Dangerous goods on this lane need a carrier approval. You'll have a price by 2pm tomorrow."

All three tell the importer they're being looked after. Two of them take less than five minutes.

How to get there

Triage on arrival. Look at every request when it lands and put it in one of the three buckets above. The one-minute triage rule sets this out in more detail.

Put the rates in one place. If pricing a standard 40HC means opening four files, it won't happen between meetings. One sheet, with valid dates.

Template the structure. Same reply layout every time: summary line, line items, total, validity, GST treatment, exclusions. There's a reply template and the six questions to ask when details are missing.

Share the inbox, not the job. If quote requests go to one person's mailbox, they wait when that person is out. A shared quotes address, with a rule that whoever's free picks it up, removes the bottleneck.

Measure it. For two weeks, log the time each request arrived and the time your first reply went out. Most owners are surprised. The average hides the requests that came in at 4.30pm and were answered at 10am the next day.

Draft automatically. The biggest gain is not having to write the quote at all, only check it. When the email is read and the rates are looked up for you, a standard quote becomes a two-minute review.

Don't trade accuracy for speed

A fast wrong quote is worse than a slow right one. If you price a shipment on gross weight instead of chargeable weight, or forget the destination CFS minimum, you either eat the difference or explain it, and the explanation costs more trust than the speed won. Every shortcut above keeps a person checking the numbers before they go out. The speed comes from removing the typing and searching, not the checking.
