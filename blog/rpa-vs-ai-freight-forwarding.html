RPA vs AI in freight forwarding: where each fits in the back office

23 November 2026 · By the Laneworks team · All articles

In short: RPA follows fixed steps on screens and files that look the same every time. AI reads messy input, like a customer's quote-request email, and turns it into clean fields. Most forwarding back-office work needs both: AI to read, fixed rules to price and post, and a person for anything outside the rules.

RPA vs AI in freight forwarding is not a choice between old and new. They solve different problems. RPA (robotic process automation) is software that repeats the clicks and keystrokes a person would make. AI, in the sense most forwarders mean today, is a language model that can read an email or a document the way a person would. This guide covers what each one is, where each fits in a forwarder's back office, how each one fails, and how to combine them safely.

What RPA is

An RPA bot is a recorded set of steps. Log in to the carrier portal, open the tracking page, copy the ETA, paste it into the job in your forwarding system, save. It does exactly what it was told, in the same order, every time.

That makes it good at work where the input and the screens never change:

  • Copying shipment status from carrier or terminal portals into job files.
  • Downloading invoices or arrival notices from a portal into a folder.
  • Keying data that is already structured (a CSV, a fixed form) into another system.
  • Running the same report every morning and emailing it out.

RPA does not understand what it is looking at. It finds a field by its position or label and acts on it.

What AI reading is

AI reading means a language model takes unstructured text, such as an email, a PDF or a table pasted into a message, and pulls out the meaning. "2 x 40HC FOB Ningbo, door to Dandenong, ready 2 Dec" becomes origin, destination, equipment, Incoterm and ready date, even though no two customers write it the same way.

That makes it good at the front of the back office, where people write the input:

  • Reading customer quote requests from email, in any layout or wording.
  • Spotting what is missing (no weight, no Incoterm, no delivery postcode) and writing the question.
  • Pulling fields from commercial invoices, packing lists and arrival notices.
  • Drafting replies in plain English for a person to check.

Where each fits in a forwarder's back office

  • Quote requests: AI. Customers write every request differently, so a recorded bot cannot read them. The price itself should come from your rate sheet by fixed rules, not from the AI.
  • Rate lookups and pricing: fixed rules. Freight by lane and equipment, local charges by Incoterm, LCL by W/M, air by chargeable weight. This is arithmetic on a table, and it should give the same answer every time. See how to structure a freight rate sheet.
  • Tracking and status updates: RPA, or an API where the carrier offers one. Same portal, same fields, every day.
  • Document data entry: AI to read the document, then RPA or an integration to key the fields into your system.
  • Exceptions: a person. New lanes, reefer, dangerous goods, oversize and claims need judgement and a partner rate.

How RPA fails

RPA depends on things staying the same. A carrier redesigns its portal, adds a new login step, renames a column in a report, or a customer sends a file in a new layout. The bot either stops, or worse, keeps going and puts data in the wrong field. Someone has to watch the runs and rebuild the steps each time.

How AI fails

AI fails when it is trusted with things it should not decide. A language model asked to "quote this shipment" can produce a believable number that is not on any rate sheet. It can also misread an unclear request: "20 pallets" with no dimensions, or a port name that exists in two countries. The fix is to keep the AI on reading and drafting, and to make every number come from a table you control.

Using both safely

The pattern that holds up in practice:

  1. AI reads the input and turns it into clean fields.
  2. Fixed rules price or process those fields from your own data. Same input, same output.
  3. Anything the rules cannot cover is flagged for a person, never guessed.
  4. A person checks the result before it goes out, until the error rate on your own work is low enough to switch that step to automatic.
